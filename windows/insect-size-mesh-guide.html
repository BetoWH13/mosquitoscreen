Window screen reference

Mosquito Screen Mesh Size Chart: What Each Mesh Blocks

The fastest way to choose insect screen is to match the mesh to the smallest insect you actually need to exclude. For most homes, standard window screening is built around mosquitoes and larger flies. If the problem is no-see-ums, biting midges, sand flies, or very small gnats, tighter small-insect screening may be needed.

One important caveat: mesh count is not the same as exact opening size. A label such as 18x16 or 20x20 describes the number of strands woven in each direction. The real opening also depends on strand diameter, coating, material, and weave, so use the product specification when exact opening size matters.

Quick Answer

  • 18x16: common residential insect screen; normally appropriate for mosquitoes and larger flying insects.
  • 20x20: tighter small-insect screen; commonly used where gnats, midges, sand flies, or no-see-ums are a problem.
  • 30x30: extra-fine specialty screening sometimes recommended where very small biting midges pass through standard screen.
  • Fit still matters: a perfect mesh choice cannot compensate for torn corners, loose frames, lifted adhesive, or open doors.

What Mesh Should You Use for Mosquitoes?

For ordinary household mosquito exclusion, standard residential insect screening is usually the starting point. CDC mosquito-control guidance notes that ordinary window screening in the 16x16 or 14x18 range keeps out most mosquitoes when it fits tightly and stays in good repair. The same guidance notes that tighter mesh can be useful where smaller mosquito species are a concern.

Modern residential fiberglass screening is also commonly sold in an 18x16 weave. If mosquitoes are still getting indoors through a screened window, inspect the perimeter and corners before assuming the mesh itself is too open.

What Mesh Should You Use for No-See-Ums and Biting Midges?

This is where ordinary mosquito screening can fail. University of Florida guidance says regular window screens do not reliably exclude biting midges. University of Arizona guidance is even more specific: no-see-ums can pass through standard 16x16 or 18x16 screening and recommends smaller mesh such as 30x30 where practical.

A second useful reference point is 20x20. University of Kentucky identifies 20x20 mesh with fine wire as a common screen size for controlling smaller midges and gnats, while major screen manufacturers sell 20x20 products specifically for small insects such as no-see-ums, gnats, and sand flies.

The practical takeaway: if your problem is a normal mosquito, start with standard residential screening and a good seal. If your problem is a tiny biting midge or no-see-um, shop specifically for small-insect or no-see-um screen rather than assuming every mosquito screen is equivalent.

Why Mesh Count Alone Can Mislead You

Two products labeled 20x20 do not necessarily have identical openings. A thicker strand occupies more space inside each square than a thinner strand. Coatings and weave construction also change the usable opening. This is why a trustworthy product sheet should tell you more than the mesh count alone.

When comparing products, check four things:

  • Mesh count: how many strands are woven in each direction.
  • Strand or wire diameter: thicker strands make the actual opening smaller at the same mesh count.
  • Material: fiberglass, aluminum, polyester, and coated products behave differently.
  • Manufacturer's intended use: look for explicit small-insect or no-see-um protection when that is the problem you are solving.

Tighter Mesh Has a Cost: Airflow and Visibility

Finer screening gives small insects fewer opportunities to pass through, but it also puts more material in the opening. Screen manufacturers explicitly treat insect protection, airflow, visibility, durability, and sun control as tradeoffs rather than promising one mesh that maximizes everything.

That means the best screen is not automatically the tightest one. Choose the least restrictive mesh that reliably handles the insects you actually have, then make sure it is installed without perimeter gaps.

If Mosquitoes Still Get Through, Check the Screen Before Replacing It

A standard mesh that should stop mosquitoes can still fail as a complete barrier if the installation is weak. Before buying finer mesh, inspect:

  • Frame perimeter: even a narrow edge gap bypasses the mesh completely.
  • Corners: look for tears, pulled spline, lifted hook-and-loop tape, or separated magnetic strips.
  • Screen tension: badly sagging mesh can pull away from the frame.
  • Doors: mosquitoes often enter while people move through unscreened or slowly closing doors.
  • Other openings: vents, damaged screens, and adjacent windows may be the real route.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will 18x16 screen mesh stop mosquitoes?

In most ordinary residential situations, yes. 18x16 is a common residential insect-screen weave intended for mosquitoes and larger flying insects. A tight fit and intact screen are essential, and smaller mosquito species can justify tighter screening.

Will standard window screen stop no-see-ums?

Not reliably. Multiple university extension sources note that tiny biting midges can pass through normal residential screening. Purpose-built small-insect or no-see-um mesh is the better category to shop.

Is 20x20 mesh better than 18x16?

It is better only when your priority is excluding smaller insects. It is tighter, but tighter screening can reduce airflow and visibility. If ordinary mosquitoes are the only problem, standard residential mesh may already be the better balance.

Is 30x30 always necessary for no-see-ums?

No. Products and local insect sizes vary. Some small-insect screens use 20x20 construction, while University of Arizona guidance recommends 30x30 where no-see-ums pass through standard screens. Buy by the manufacturer's stated pest/application and technical specification, not mesh count alone.

Does darker screen block smaller insects better?

Color is not the controlling factor. Mesh geometry, strand diameter, material, installation quality, and actual opening size determine exclusion. Color mainly affects appearance and visibility.
